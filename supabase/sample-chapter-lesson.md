Tides shape every shoreline you will trace. This short lesson walks through what causes them, how to read them on the shore, and how to record what you see.

## Why tides happen
The moon's gravity pulls on the ocean and makes the water bulge on the side facing it. A second bulge forms on the far side of the Earth, so most coasts see **two high tides and two low tides** each day.

- The sun adds a smaller pull of its own.
- The shape of the coast changes how high the water climbs.
- Wind and air pressure can push the tide higher or lower than predicted.

## Spring and neap tides
When the sun, moon and Earth line up, their pulls add together.

1. **Spring tides** happen at new and full moon. High tides are higher and low tides are lower.
2. **Neap tides** happen at the quarter moons. The pulls partly cancel, so the range is small.

Plan shoreline visits for a low spring tide if you want to see the most exposed ground.

## Reading the shore
Three signs tell you what the last tide did:

1. **Waterline.** The edge of the wet sand. Note how far it sits from the dry sand.
2. **Wrack line.** The row of seaweed and debris left at the highest reach of the last tide.
3. **Ripple marks.** Small ridges in the sand that show which way the water moved.

### What to photograph
Include the wrack line and something for scale, such as a shoe or a ruler.

## Recording a trace
Open **Submit a trace**, add a clear title, and describe what you saw in a sentence or two. Add the time of your visit, because the same spot looks very different at high and low tide.

Finish with a photo and your location, then save a draft or submit for review.
